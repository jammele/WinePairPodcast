## Headline / CTR research

**Question headlines generally outperform statement headlines, but not always.**
A study published in *Social Influence* found non-self-referencing question headlines were clicked ~137% more often than statement headlines on average, rising to ~257% more for self-referencing question headlines. Mechanism: questions stimulate speculation about the answer, which drives curiosity-based clicks. **Domain: written article headlines, not podcast titles or podcast-app browsing UI. Not independently re-verified in this revision pass — treat as directional only.**
Source: [Are you more likely to click headlines that are phrased as a question? — BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/are-you-more-likely-click-headlines-are-phrased-question)

**Counterpoint: weak/vague questions can underperform strong, specific statements.**
In direct-response marketing contexts, headlines making a clear, specific promise generated stronger engagement and conversions than open-ended questions. Question format for a washing-machine headline actually led to fewer clicks than a statement. Takeaway: the win isn't "question > statement" unconditionally — it's "concrete and specific > vague," and a vague question can lose to a concrete statement. **Domain: direct-response marketing copy, not podcast titles. Not independently re-verified this pass — treat as directional only.**
Source: [Copy Tip: The Deadly "?" — Why Question Headlines Often Hurt Response Rates](https://cdmginc.com/2026/05/20/copy-tip-the-deadly-why-question-headlines-often-hurt-response-rates/)

**Banerjee and Urminsky: evidence that wording can affect web-headline clicks, not a podcast-title formula.**

The final published study analyzed thousands of headline field experiments conducted on Upworthy and found that textual characteristics can causally affect click-through to online articles. Its published abstract also indicates that prior research and industry advice did not consistently predict the direction of those effects.

The final full text was not available for review. An earlier 2021 working paper was explicitly labeled preliminary and warned that its confirmatory results were likely to differ. Therefore, this reference does not extract or preserve any directional title-writing guidance from that preliminary version.

**Permitted takeaway:** wording can affect clicks in web-headline experiments, and those effects can be context-dependent.

**Not permitted:** using this study to score, rank, recommend, reject, or generate a Wine Pair Podcast title; claiming that it validates a particular level of concreteness, question structure, curiosity technique, or title formula; or transferring its web-article results to podcast applications.

Source: the final published journal record and abstract at the INFORMS DOI page (https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2021.0018). The preliminary working paper (https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf) may be linked only to explain why its directional findings are not being used.

A previous version of this file incorrectly attributed an inverted-U concreteness finding to this study. That attribution has been removed and must not be repeated.

**Negative/superlative framing — reported finding, NOT verifiable from a live primary source as of 2026-08-21. Do not cite the percentage figure in active guidance.**
Previously cited in this file: "Outbrain's analysis of ~65,000 headlines found negative-superlative framing out-CTRs positive framing by 63%." Verification attempted this session: the original Outbrain blog post — `https://www.outbrain.com/blog/headlines-when-the-best-brings-the-worst-and-the-worst-brings-the-best` — returned HTTP 404, and this session's tools could not retrieve an archived version (Wayback Machine access is unavailable to this session's tooling). The figure is independently repeated with consistent numbers (63% CTR lift; component figures of +30% for negative superlatives and -29% for positive superlatives vs. neutral) by a marketing blog (ignitevisibility.com) and by Poynter (a respected journalism/media-research organization) — both cite Outbrain as the original source, and both point to the same now-dead URL. This pattern is consistent with a real historical Outbrain publication that has since been taken down or moved, but it cannot be confirmed from a primary source with the tools available this session.
**Action taken per house rule: the 63% (and the 29%/30% component) figures are removed from active title guidance.** They are recorded here only as an unverifiable historical citation. Do not cite these percentages in any title recommendation, subagent output, or session report. If a future session locates a live primary source (an Outbrain-hosted page, a press release, or an independent replication with disclosed methodology), the figure may be reinstated with that citation and the "verified" status updated accordingly.
Sources checked (2026-08-21): [outbrain.com/blog/headlines-when-the-best-brings-the-worst-and-the-worst-brings-the-best](https://www.outbrain.com/blog/headlines-when-the-best-brings-the-worst-and-the-worst-brings-the-best) — 404, unreachable; [ignitevisibility.com secondary citation](https://ignitevisibility.com/7-science-backed-secrets-improve-copywriting/) — secondary, cites the same dead URL; [Poynter secondary corroboration](https://www.poynter.org/news/worst-news-ever-negative-headlines-outperform-positive-ones) — journalistic corroboration, no independent methodology disclosed, also cites the same dead URL.

**Curiosity-gap theory (Loewenstein) — conceptual grounding only, never a scoring input.**
Curiosity requires a felt gap between what the reader knows and what they want to know. A headline referencing something the target reader has no context for (a niche comparison wine, an inside term) doesn't create a felt gap for a new listener — it just reads as unfamiliar. This is the theoretical basis for HR-66 (no insider/prior-listener context required). This is a long-established theoretical framework (information-gap theory of curiosity), not a single study with a specific statistic — it is retained here purely as conceptual grounding for *why* HR-66 matters, and must never be used as a numeric or scoring input.

## AEO (answer engine optimization) for podcasts — 2026 guidance — vendor sources, unverified hypothesis, low-confidence background

**Named-entity titles + answer-oriented framing: an unverified vendor claim, not a confirmed finding.**
Vendor/content-marketing sources claim that AEO practice favors episode/chapter titles that pair a named entity (the grape, wine, or brand) with an answer-oriented question structure ("What is X?", "How does Y work?"), and that pages citing structured transcripts, named-entity titles, FAQPage-style schema, AudioObject markup, and chapter-level timestamps are cited by AI Overview more often than pages without them. **A specific multiplier for that citation-frequency claim has appeared in vendor material but has not been independently verified from a primary or measurable source and is not carried into active guidance — do not cite any specific "cited X times more often" figure in title guidance, a session report, or a recommendation to Joe.** These sources have no disclosed methodology and are the only podcast-specific sources in this file — kept as a low-confidence, unvalidated hypothesis because nothing better-sourced is available for this specific question, not because the claim itself has been confirmed. "The title is one input among several" should likewise be treated as an unverified vendor claim, not an established fact.
Source: [How to Optimize Your Podcast for AEO in 2026](https://www.bravemoonpodcasts.com/post/how-to-optimize-your-podcast-for-aeo-answer-engine-optimization-in-2026), [Podcast SEO 2026: AEO Citation Strategy](https://forkoff.xyz/blog/podcasts/podcast-aeo-citation-strategy-2026)

**Practical implication for comparing title candidates on AEO grounds:**
When candidate titles contain the same named entity and topic language, no verified evidence currently establishes a material AEO difference among them. Report "no evidence-based differentiation" rather than claiming either an AEO benefit or a measured AEO wash.

**Whether show notes and schema outweigh the title for AEO purposes is an unverified hypothesis, not a confirmed finding — and must not be used to differentiate or recommend episode titles.**
Vendor sources claim that Key Questions, FAQPage-equivalent Q&A blocks, and structured show notes (already produced via `/generate-episode-content` per house rules) matter more for AI Overview / ChatGPT / Perplexity citation than the episode title does. This claim has not been independently validated against this show's own data or any measurable outcome, and it must never be used as a reason to prefer, drop, or rank one candidate title over another — this show has no verified AEO performance data at the title level at all.

## Corpus methodology — how to use the show's own title archive as evidence

The "Internal benchmark data" section that previously named Meiomi as the standing comparison point for every title session is retired. **No episode, and no fixed or rotating set of episodes, replaces it as a standing reference.** Use this process instead:

**The corpus is the full archive, not a curated list.** `data/title-session-reports.md` (episode entries + Patterns Learned) and `data/episode-titles.md` (complete title list with format annotations) are searchable in full each session. Nothing in this file maintains a pre-selected exemplar list, and none should be added.

**Select past examples only when structurally relevant to the current episode.** Match on: format (review / interview / comparison / roundup), familiarity level (well-known brand or region vs. unfamiliar grape), named-series status, subject type, and title structure (declarative-opener vs. question-closer, single-clause vs. multi-clause). An episode with no structural match to the current one should not be cited, no matter how often it's been cited in the past.

**Counterexamples are required only when an internal pattern is cited as evidence for a recommendation — not for every title and not for routine hard-rule decisions.** If a title-generation note or a subagent recommendation treats a past episode's outcome as support for an approach ("this structure has worked before"), check the archive for a plausible counterexample — a structurally similar past episode that wasn't selected, was rejected, or landed differently. If a meaningful counterexample exists, name it. If a real check turns up none, say so briefly ("no meaningful counterexample found in the archive") rather than silently skipping the check. Hard-rule PASS/FAIL determinations (HR-15, HR-16, HR-17, HR-18, HR-39, etc.) are rule-compliance checks, not pattern citations, and don't require this step.

**Call something a "corpus-level pattern" only when the underlying fields actually support that conclusion.** A pattern claim (e.g., "named-series titles with an added subtitle tend to need more review rounds") must be checked against enough archive entries to say so honestly. If only one or two entries exist, describe it as an observation from those specific episodes — not a corpus-level pattern. Do not round up from a couple of anecdotes to "the archive shows."

**Review-round counts are process evidence, not listener-performance evidence.** How many rounds a title took, what got FAILed and why, and what changed between rounds tells you about the generation/review process — not about how listeners would have responded. Never present "this title passed cleanly in one round" as evidence it would perform well, or "this title took three rounds" as evidence it would perform poorly.

**Joe's documented selections and overrides are editorial evidence, not listener-performance evidence.** When Joe selects the recommendation, picks a lower-scored option, combines options, writes his own title, or rejects a batch, that's genuinely useful evidence about the show's voice and Joe's editorial judgment — but it is not proof of what a listener would click, download, or play. Keep these two evidence types labeled separately; never blend an editorial-preference finding into a performance claim.

**No performance statement without identified outcome data.** Do not say a title "worked," "performed well," "drove clicks," or similar without naming the specific metric, source, and window behind the claim (see Evidence types below). No outcome data exists for nearly all episodes in the archive — say so plainly rather than inferring performance from a title's structure, a topic's general popularity, or a review-round count.

## Evidence types — kept separate, never blended

| Evidence type | What it actually measures | What it does NOT measure |
|---|---|---|
| **Apple Podcasts data** (Apple Podcasts Connect analytics) | Metrics such as listeners, engaged listeners, and plays, within a stated window | **Followers are a show-level metric, not an episode-level one — a follower-count change cannot be attributed to any single episode or that episode's title.** Episode-level listens/plays still cannot isolate the title's contribution from cover art, description, or release timing. |
| **Spotify data** (Spotify for Podcasters analytics) | Spotify's own named metrics — impressions, plays, conversion rate, completion rate, and average completion rate — plus discovery source (e.g. Home / Search / Library), within a stated window. **See the Spotify metric definitions immediately below this table — conversion rate, completion rate, and average completion rate are three distinct metrics and must never be used interchangeably.** | Which specific factor (title, cover art, description, topic, release timing) caused a given number — no isolated title effect exists in this data |
| **Buzzsprout download data** | Episode downloads, within a stated window | Whether the title specifically drove the number — download data does not isolate a title effect. (No claim is made about whether downloads reflect RSS/subscriber behavior more or less than any other discovery mechanism; that comparison has not been verified and should not be asserted.) |
| **GSC (Google Search Console) website evidence** | Organic Google search clicks, impressions, and CTR to a specific webpage (e.g. an episode's page on thewinepairpodcast.com) for specific search queries, within a stated window | **This is not podcast-app title clickthrough.** It reflects the webpage's title tag, meta description, and on-page SEO ranking for search queries — a different surface and different user intent than someone browsing a podcast app. This is the exact conflation that produced the retired Meiomi benchmark: "241 clicks / 1.18% CTR" (see `docs/seo-geo-strategy.md`, `docs/work-log.md:1112`) was GSC data for the Meiomi episode *webpage*, not podcast-title clickthrough. |
| **YouTube data** | View counts and engagement on competitor or reference videos found during title research (Step 0.5) | Title causation — a video's view count reflects channel authority, topic demand, thumbnail, and algorithm placement at least as much as its title wording. Never cite a YouTube view count as evidence a title "worked." |
| **General headline research** (Banerjee/Urminsky; BPS/Social Influence; CDMG; the retired Outbrain figure) | Aggregate patterns from web-article or ad-copy headline experiments/studies | Podcast-title performance specifically. None of these studies were conducted on podcast titles or podcast-app browsing behavior. Banerjee/Urminsky is non-operational and may not be used to generate, score, rank, recommend, or reject titles, as specified above. Any other retained headline research is qualitative background only, never a numeric input or proof of podcast-title performance. |
| **Joe's editorial decisions** (selections, overrides, combinations, self-written titles, batch rejections) | The show's actual voice and editorial judgment, as demonstrated by a real decision-maker | Listener response — Joe's preference is not a proxy for audience behavior, even though it is the single most reliable evidence this workflow has access to for voice and framing questions |

**Spotify metric definitions — verified against Spotify's official documentation (2026-08-21). These three are distinct and must never be used interchangeably:**
- **Conversion rate:** "The percentage of impressions that led to plays on Spotify." Available in both show-level and episode-level Discovery analytics. When citing it, identify which view supplied the figure and preserve its displayed window. Updates every 48 hours. (Spotify's Sharing-analytics view defines a separate "clicks that led to plays" conversion rate for the sharing context specifically — if citing a conversion-rate figure, confirm which of the two contexts it came from.)
- **Completion rate:** "The percentage of your audience that made it at least 95% of the way through an episode on Spotify during its first 7 days after publish." Episode-level. First-7-days-after-publish window. Updates every 24 hours.
- **Average completion rate:** "The percentage of your audience that made it at least 95% of the way through an episode. This rate is averaged across recent episodes of this show on Spotify that have 7 days of data." Show-level aggregate across multiple episodes, each with 7 days of data. Updates every 24 hours.
Source: [Spotify Analytics Glossary](https://support.spotify.com/us/creators/article/analytics-glossary/), [Understanding Discovery Analytics](https://support.spotify.com/us/creators/article/discovery-analytics/).

**Before citing any Spotify figure, state:** (1) the exact displayed metric name (e.g. "completion rate," not "completion"); (2) whether it is episode-level or show-level; (3) the applicable time window. Completion rate (one episode, first 7 days) and average completion rate (a show-wide rolling average across several episodes) are never interchangeable — citing one when the other is meant is a factual error, not a rounding choice.

**None of the evidence types above currently isolates the title's causal effect on listener behavior.** No available data source separates a title's contribution from topic familiarity, cover art, description, release timing, or platform placement. Treat every performance-adjacent claim in this file, in `data/title-session-reports.md`, or in any session output as correlational at best, with the confounds named — never as proof that a specific title choice caused a specific outcome.
