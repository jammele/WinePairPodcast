## Headline / CTR research

**Question headlines generally outperform statement headlines, but not always.**
A study published in *Social Influence* found non-self-referencing question headlines were clicked ~137% more often than statement headlines on average, rising to ~257% more for self-referencing question headlines. Mechanism: questions stimulate speculation about the answer, which drives curiosity-based clicks.
Source: [Are you more likely to click headlines that are phrased as a question? — BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/are-you-more-likely-click-headlines-are-phrased-question)

**Counterpoint: weak/vague questions can underperform strong, specific statements.**
In direct-response marketing contexts, headlines making a clear, specific promise generated stronger engagement and conversions than open-ended questions. Question format for a washing-machine headline actually led to fewer clicks than a statement. Takeaway: the win isn't "question > statement" unconditionally — it's "concrete and specific > vague," and a vague question can lose to a concrete statement.
Source: [Copy Tip: The Deadly "?" — Why Question Headlines Often Hurt Response Rates](https://cdmginc.com/2026/05/20/copy-tip-the-deadly-why-question-headlines-often-hurt-response-rates/)

**Negative/superlative framing out-performs positive framing.**
Outbrain's analysis of ~65,000 headlines found negative-superlative framing out-CTRs positive framing by 63%. This is the same mechanism behind the show's own internal Meiomi benchmark (see below).
Source: cited via [7 Science-Backed Secrets to Improve Your Copywriting](https://ignitevisibility.com/7-science-backed-secrets-improve-copywriting/)

**"Optimal concreteness" — curiosity gaps need to be calibrated, not maximized.**
A large-scale meta-analysis of ~9,000 headline experiments (Banerjee & Urminsky, University of Chicago) found headline performance follows an inverted-U relative to concreteness: too vague underperforms because it doesn't give the reader enough to be curious about; too resolved underperforms because there's no gap left. The best-performing headlines sit in a middle "optimal concreteness" zone — specific enough to create a real, answerable question, not so specific that the answer is already given away.
Source: [A Systematic Large-Scale Analysis of Headline Experiments — Banerjee/Urminsky, U Chicago](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf)

**Curiosity-gap theory (Loewenstein) — a gap only registers against what the reader already knows.**
Curiosity requires a felt gap between what the reader knows and what they want to know. A headline referencing something the target reader has no context for (e.g., a niche comparison wine, an inside term) doesn't create a felt gap for a new listener — it just reads as unfamiliar. This is the theoretical basis for HR-66 (no insider/prior-listener context required).

## AEO (answer engine optimization) for podcasts — 2026 guidance

**Named-entity titles + answer-oriented framing drive AI citation.**
Current AEO practice favors episode/chapter titles that pair a named entity (the grape, wine, or brand) with an answer-oriented question structure ("What is X?", "How does Y work?"). AI Overview cites podcast pages 2-3x more often than generic blog posts when the page also ships structured transcripts, named-entity titles, FAQPage-style schema, AudioObject markup, and chapter-level timestamps — the title alone is one input among several, not the dominant lever.
Source: [How to Optimize Your Podcast for AEO in 2026](https://www.bravemoonpodcasts.com/post/how-to-optimize-your-podcast-for-aeo-answer-engine-optimization-in-2026), [Podcast SEO 2026: AEO Citation Strategy](https://forkoff.xyz/blog/podcasts/podcast-aeo-citation-strategy-2026)

**Practical implication for comparing title candidates on AEO grounds:**
When multiple candidate titles all contain the same named entity (e.g., "Vermentino") and the same topical phrase (e.g., "summer sipper"), AEO discoverability is effectively a wash between them — the AEO benefit comes from the entity/topic being present at all, not from which exact subtitle wording is chosen. Don't manufacture an AEO differentiation between similar candidates that doesn't actually exist; say so plainly when that's the honest finding (as with Ep229's three finalist options).

**The real AEO lever for this show is the show notes and schema, not the episode title.**
Key Questions, FAQPage-equivalent Q&A blocks, and structured show notes (already produced via `/generate-episode-content` per house rules) are what most directly drives AI Overview / ChatGPT / Perplexity citation, per the sources above. The title's job for AEO purposes is mainly to carry the named entity — its job for human clickability is a separate, and often more decisive, concern for a title-selection session.

## Internal benchmark data (The Wine Pair Podcast)

**Meiomi: "The Worst Wine We've Ever Tasted?"** — 241 clicks, the highest CTR of any brand episode to date. Negative-superlative framing + a withheld verdict (question mark) + genuine controversy appeal to both fans and skeptics. This remains the strongest internal data point for what drives clicks on this specific show, and should be the first comparison point for any new title's controversy/curiosity-gap potential.
