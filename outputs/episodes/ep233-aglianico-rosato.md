### FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS
*(Place at bottom of show notes)*

**Q. If you like a bold red wine, should you try the rosé made from the same grape?**
A. Yes, it's worth trying. A rosé (rosato in Italian) made from the same grape as a red you love, like Aglianico, keeps a version of that grape's real character, just lighter, since brief skin contact still shapes the flavor. It won't guarantee every bottle is great, but the logic holds especially for bold, high-tannin reds.

**Q. What does Aglianico rosé (rosato) taste like?**
A. It varies a lot bottle to bottle. One version tasted like strawberries and cream with orange creamsicle and lemon sorbet notes, light and crisp. Another leaned grapefruit and cocktail-like, more acidic and tea-tinged. Both landed firmer and more savory than a typical pale rosé.

**Q. Why does a bold red grape like Aglianico make such a distinctive rosé?**
A. Aglianico is naturally high in tannin, one of Italy's boldest red grapes. Even a few hours of skin contact pulls in enough tannin to give the rosé real structure and brighter color than typical pale rosé, plus a drier, more savory character instead of light fruit alone.

**Q. What food pairs well with Aglianico rosé?**
A. It's food-friendly across a wide range. A lighter, fruit-forward style works well with fried fish, shellfish, octopus, smoked salmon, deviled eggs, and brunch dishes like eggs benedict. A drier, more acidic style pairs better with bready foods like focaccia, calzone, or white pizza. Match the wine's weight to what you're eating.

**Q. Is rosé wine just a blend of red and white wine?**
A. No, that's a common misconception. Real rosé gets its pink color from letting red grape skins sit briefly with the juice, not from mixing red and white wine together. Some rosé comes from bleeding off juice during red wine production, called saignée, but most quality rosé today is made intentionally as its own wine, not a byproduct.
