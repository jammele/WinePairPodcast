And now it is time to head over to our news desk so that we can cover our wine in the news this week segment.

This week our wine in the news comes to us from Anna Sulkin Stern writing for Wealth Management. And the article is called "Estate Dispute Sees Potentially Largest Sanctions Imposed For AI-Related Misconduct."

Right away, this feels like a wild story, and the short version of the story is this: a woman tried to sue her brothers over the family winery, and her lawyers submitted fake AI-generated court cases to the judge. Fifteen of them. And somehow that wasn't the most unhinged part.

Now let me back up, because there are some details here that are too good to skip.

The story starts with a family in Jacksonville, Oregon. Their mother passes away, and she leaves behind Valley View Winery. And you can probably guess where this goes. The siblings cannot agree on who should be running things. So the sister, Joanne Couvrette, decides to sue her brothers, Mark and Michael Wisnovsky, for twelve million dollars.

Now, when you file a lawsuit, your lawyers submit legal briefs. These are documents that cite actual court cases, actual legal precedents, actual things that really happened in a real courtroom somewhere. That is how this is supposed to work.

But wait, it gets more interesting.

Joanne's legal team submitted briefs to the judge containing fifteen AI-generated fake case citations and eight fabricated quotations. Not one. Fifteen. Across three separate filings. Over five months. Some of the cases they cited did not exist at all. Others cited real cases but made up what those cases actually said.

The judge caught on. Judge Clarke dismissed Joanne's claims entirely and imposed over one hundred thousand dollars in sanctions against the two attorneys involved, which is being described as potentially the largest penalty ever imposed in the United States for AI-related misconduct in a legal filing.

But wait, it gets more interesting.

Here is where it really goes sideways. The evidence in this case suggests that Joanne herself may have written the AI-generated briefs and then handed them to her lawyers to submit. So to review: she may have used AI to fabricate her own legal arguments, given them to her attorneys, and those attorneys submitted them to a federal judge without checking whether any of it was real.

She is planning to appeal.

Carmela, I have to ask you this. If we ever had a family business and things went sideways, and you decided you were going to take it to court -- is there any version of this where you hand me a stack of documents and say "just file these, don't worry about it"?
