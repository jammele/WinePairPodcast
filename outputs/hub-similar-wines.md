# If You Like This Wine, Try That: Your Guide to Finding Similar Wines You'll Love

Do you remember that moment when you found a wine you fell in love with? The wine that made you so crazy about wine that you couldn't wait until you had it again? Do you also ever run into the situation where you can't find the exact wine you are looking for, and you are standing in a store in front of several hundred bottles you've never heard of, or starting at the wine list at a restaurant, or at a dinner party with wines to choose from but no idea what to choose, and realize you have absolutely no idea how to find something like that wine you love?

That's the moment most wine drinkers hit. And it's the moment when most of us wish we had some sort of cheat sheet, in our heads or in our pockets, to help us find a wine that can give us a similar experience to the wine we love. It is also the moment where we find ourselves just drinking the same one or two wines that we always drink because we don't know what else to drink.

We don't blame anyone who finds themselves in that situation for staying safe. The wine world is not designed to help casual drinkers find their way. Big wine brands and distributors want us to drink the few wines that they make and sell. The labels of foreign wines reward expertise you don't have. The bottles are grouped by region or by grape or by whatever the distributor paid for shelf space, none of which tells you whether a wine will feel anything like the one you loved. Staff recommendations are inconsistent and the questions embarrassing. And when you do take a chance and it doesn't work, you're out $20+ and freshly reminded why you stick to what you know. Bad wine experiences are really disappointing, not just financially but psychologically. They train you to stop trying. Over time, most people end up sticking to what they know and never bothering to stray.

That's the problem every guide on this page was built to fix. We're Joe and Carmela Mele, the independent husband-and-wife team behind The Wine Pair Podcast, recommended by Decanter Magazine, named one of Ear Worthy's Best Independent Podcasts of 2026, and a Top 100 Food Podcast on Apple. We've released more than 225 episodes, tasted more than 500 wines on the podcast, and we buy all of the wine we review with our own money. No free samples or sponsorships, and never from wineries or wine brands. Our goal is to give our listeners our unvarnished and honest feelings and information about the wines we drink. And these "find similar wine" guides are meant to do the same. Be a trusted source for you.

## What "Similar" Actually Means in Wine

Most people, when they look for a wine similar to one they love, are thinking about flavor. That seems logical. It's also why the search keeps failing.

Flavor and style descriptions in wine are often confusing and seem to lack the precision you want. "Cherry" appears on the tasting notes of a pale, light-bodied Beaujolais and a deep, extracted Amarone. They are completely different wines. "Fruity" describes a $9 sweet rosé and a serious Grenache from the Southern Rhone. Dry wines range from crisp, acidic whites to heavy, oaky California Cabs. The words sound the same; the wines do not drink the same. Chasing flavor or style descriptors will steer you toward the wrong bottle more often than not.

What actually makes two wines feel similar when you drink them is structure: body (how it feels in your mouth, from light and watery to rich and heavy), tannin (the drying, chalky grip on your gums, ranging from almost none in Beaujolais to substantial in Cabernet Sauvignon), acidity (the brightness that makes your mouth water and keeps a wine from tasting flat), and how the fruit is weighted (light and fresh up front, or dense and dark and slow to open). Wines that share those structural markers will feel like natural companions even when they taste different on the surface. Wines that look similar on a label but diverge on structure will disappoint you every time.

Each of the six guides below was built around these structural markers, drawn from real episodes where we tasted the wines, discussed and deconstructed them, talked through what connects them to other wines, explored what foods they pair with well, and gave them really honest ratings and reviews. These guides were not assembled from generic wine knowledge. They were written because after five years of podcasting and 125,000+ downloads across 53 countries, we know exactly which questions wine drinkers actually ask, and what they need to hear in order to actually try something new.

## If You Love Pinot Noir

Pinot Noir drinkers are among the most loyal in the wine world, and there's a reason for that. What they love is more than a flavor. It's a feeling. Pinot has a lightness and an elegance that makes it feel effortless with food, and restrained in the mouth. The tannins are soft and forgiving, which is part of why it works so well at the table. The cherry and earth notes are refined and complex, and worth slowing down for.

Pinot is also, for a lot of drinkers, the wine that first made them take wine seriously. It can also be expensive. The grape is famously finicky, prone to rot, only really expressive in a handful of climates, and so is often priced out of the reach of many drinkers.

Our guide covers six wines that share Pinot's structural DNA without pretending to be Pinot. Mencía from northwestern Spain is the closest structural match we found: medium body, medium tannin, high acidity, floral and mineral on the finish, regularly available under $20, and the wine that Pinot drinkers tend to fall for hardest because it feels familiar and surprising at the same time. Beaujolais, made from Gamay rather than Pinot Noir but cut from the same cloth in terms of weight and brightness, delivers the same food-friendliness and cherry character at the most approachable price on the list. Frappato from Sicily goes even lighter and more floral, the choice for anyone who loves Pinot at its most delicate. Cinsault from southern France or South Africa brings the same soft, nearly weightless texture. Etna Rosso from Mount Etna is the most interesting lateral step: Pinot's volcanic Sicilian neighbor, same weight class, sharper acidity, and a mineral quality from the soil that you won't find anywhere else on this list. And Red Burgundy, the original, is where all of this leads eventually.

## If You Love Malbec

A lot of people's real wine journey starts with Malbec, and it makes sense. Malbec is bold without being punishing. Dark-fruited, smooth, and available at almost every price point from $9 to $35. It overdelivers at the $12 to $18 range more consistently than almost any other red we cover on the podcast. The tannin is firm enough to feel serious without the tight, drying grip that can make other bold reds feel like work.

The problem Malbec creates is a pleasant one: it's so reliably good that it's hard to leave. We hear this from listeners constantly. They've been buying the same Malbec for years, they know they love it, and they genuinely want to try something new but don't know how to start without risking a bad bottle and a wasted night. The guide we built for Malbec drinkers is designed to solve exactly that problem, covering wines close enough to feel safe and wines different enough to feel like real discovery.

Primitivo from Puglia is the most natural next step: genetically related to Zinfandel, structurally close to Malbec, with dried fig and black pepper where Malbec gives you blueberry and chocolate, and solid bottles regularly under $15. Nero d'Avola from Sicily shares the same dark fruit weight with a spice and herbal note that Malbec doesn't have, and it's another strong value. Grenache (Garnacha in Spain) is the move if you want to stay dark-fruited and approachable but need something more food-flexible: less tannin, warmer and fruitier, the most versatile wine on the list when it comes to pairing. Syrah is for when Malbec has started feeling predictable: all the darkness of Malbec plus black pepper, smoked meat, and a complexity that rewards a little more attention. Zinfandel is the jammy, spicier American cousin. And Pinotage from South Africa is the adventurous pick: bold, dark-fruited, with a smokiness that either defines it or divides opinions depending on how you feel about that quality in a glass.

## If You Love Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon anchors the American wine market for a reason. It delivers what a lot of drinkers want from a serious red: firm tannin, dark fruit (blackberry, cassis, cedar on the finish), and staying power. It pairs well with the foods most people actually cook and order. It exists at every price from $8 to $800. And it's consistent enough that once you find a producer you trust, you can buy without second-guessing.

What many Cab drinkers don't realize is that they're already in a larger family. Bordeaux blends are mostly Cabernet, with Merlot and sometimes Cabernet Franc or Petit Verdot blended in for complexity. When you open a Napa Meritage or a value bottle from the Medoc, you're already in Cab territory, just with a slightly different supporting cast. Our guide uses that family tree as the map.

Merlot is the most immediate step: same Bordeaux family, same dark fruit vocabulary, but softer on the tannin and more immediately drinkable without planning a steak around it. Bordeaux blends show you what Cab looks like in its natural habitat, with more complexity from the blend. Carménère is one of our favorite recommendations for Cab drinkers: grown almost exclusively in Chile, descended from the same lineage as Cabernet Franc, the same structural weight as Cab but with a distinctive green pepper and tobacco note that makes it immediately interesting and usually available under $20. Tannat from Uruguay or France is for the drinker who found Cab's tannin to be its most interesting quality and wants more of it: higher tannin levels than almost any other red, earthier and more rustic, built to age. Rioja Reserva (Tempranillo) delivers real structure and oak presence in a familiar weight class but with dried cherry, tobacco, and vanilla instead of blackberry and cedar. And Barolo, made from Nebbiolo in Piedmont, is where a lot of serious Cab drinkers eventually land: massive tannin, high acidity, dried cherry and rose and something earthy and complex that changes what you thought a bold red could be.

## If You Love Chardonnay

Chardonnay is the most misunderstood grape in the wine world, and the winemaker gets most of the blame, not the grape. The heavily oaked, almost-sweet California-style Chardonnays that turned a generation of drinkers against the grape are a stylistic choice, and a fairly aggressive one. The same grape, grown cooler with less oak, makes something completely different: lean, taut, electric.

Our Chardonnay guide works differently from the others because what Chardonnay drinkers actually love varies more than any wine we cover. Some people love the richness and the body. Some love the oak and vanilla. Some love the stone fruit and the round, generous texture. Some love all of it. The guide is structured almost like a diagnostic: figure out what you love about your Chardonnay first, and we'll point you to the right alternative.

If you love the body and stone fruit but want something that gets there without barrel aging: Viognier is the answer. Full body, peach and apricot aromatics, a round and generous texture, all from the grape itself without any oak. If you want to understand what Chardonnay actually tastes like underneath the barrel work: Chablis is 100% Chardonnay from the cool northern edge of Burgundy, completely unoaked, and a revelation if the big California style is your only reference point. White Rioja, labeled Crianza or Reserva, shows you how oak and age can build real complexity: serious character, Viura grape, different fruit story, usually under $20. For Italian alternatives, Fiano from Campania and Verdicchio from Marche both offer the body and texture Chardonnay drinkers recognize without tasting anything like Chardonnay, and both are strong values. Grenache Blanc from southern France is for anyone who wants Chardonnay's full-bodied texture in a warmer, earthier, more food-friendly package.

## If You Love Sauvignon Blanc

Sauvignon Blanc drinkers tend to know exactly what they want: crisp, dry, citrus and herbal, clean and alive. It's a specific brief, and when a Sauvignon Blanc delivers on it, it's hard to think of anything you'd rather have with grilled fish, Thai food, oysters, or anything with a bright acidic sauce.

The good news is that structural profile shows up in wines from Spain, Austria, France, Greece, Argentina, and the Basque Country, many of them relatively easy to find and in several cases better value than the Sauvignon Blancs sitting next to them on the shelf.

Albariño from Galicia in northwestern Spain is the most natural first step: same citrus brightness, same love of seafood, slightly rounder and more stone-fruit-forward with a coastal salinity that Sauvignon Blanc doesn't quite have. Grüner Veltliner from Austria is the herbal, citrus-forward option with a white pepper note that makes it immediately distinctive: a serious food wine, underrated, and one of the better values in white wine right now. Picpoul de Pinet from southern France is the lean, nervy, citrus-driven pick at the best price on the list, regularly under $15 and genuinely excellent next to oysters. Assyrtiko from Santorini is the deep end: one of the most electrically acidic white wines in the world, grown in volcanic pumice soil, mineral and saline in a way no other white quite replicates. Torrontés from Argentina is the aromatic outlier: intensely floral, rose petals and citrus blossom, dry and refreshing but with a completely different aromatics story. And Txakoli, the Basque Country's house white, is lightly fizzy, bone dry, under 11% alcohol, and one of those wines where the description alone makes people immediately curious.

## If You Love Prosecco

Most sparkling wine guides start from Champagne and work their way down to Prosecco. We think that approach is backwards, and not just in terms of price. Recommending Champagne to someone who loves Prosecco is like handing a light lager drinker a double IPA and saying "it's also beer." Technically true. Practically not useful.

Our guide starts from Prosecco and moves outward. Cava from Spain is the most natural first step: affordable, bottle-fermented (which gives it finer, more persistent bubbles than Prosecco), drier and a touch more complex, usually under $15. Crémant d'Alsace from France is the elegant alternative: fine bubbles, floral and mineral, made from Alsatian grapes, a step up in refinement from both Cava and Prosecco. Pét-Nat is for the curious: low-intervention, naturally fermented in the bottle, hazy, slightly funky in the best way, and genuinely different every time you open one. German Riesling Sekt is the wildcard, electric with acidity and baking spice. American sparkling brut from producers like Gruet in New Mexico or Treveri Cellars in Washington gives you the Champagne method (bottle fermentation, lees aging, real toasty complexity) at Prosecco pricing.

And then there's Lambrusco. It's a red sparkling wine from Emilia-Romagna in northern Italy, it pairs with charcuterie and pizza better than almost anything else we've tasted, and it was one of our highest-rated wines across the entire similar wines series. If you haven't had it, it belongs on your list. For the Prosecco drinker who wants to understand what Italy can do with sparkling wine when it's really trying: Franciacorta. Bottle-fermented, aged on the lees for at least 18 months, made from Chardonnay and Pinot Noir, and a genuine step up in complexity. It's not a Tuesday night wine, but it might become your special-occasion wine.

## What Five Years of Tasting Has Actually Taught Us

The bigger insight behind all of this isn't about individual wines. It's about how wine exploration actually works for most people.

The drinkers who stay curious, who go from knowing one wine to knowing eight or ten they genuinely love, aren't the ones who took big leaps into unfamiliar territory. They're the ones who moved in small, confident steps. They found the wine that was 80% familiar and 20% new, built confidence with that bottle, then took another step. Over time, the unfamiliar becomes familiar, the territory gets bigger, and it never feels like a risk.

Every guide in this series runs on that logic. We also built the Winedr companion app on the same idea: a personality quiz that matches you to wines based on the structural dimensions from more than 80 bottles we've tasted and rated ourselves. If these guides are the map, the podcast is the guided tour, and the app is what you use at the store when you can't remember which Albariño we liked. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. How do you find wines similar to one you like?**
A. Start with structure, not flavor. What you love about your go-to wine is probably structural: the weight, the tannin level, the acidity, how the fruit hits you. Figure that out first, and then look for wines that share those characteristics. Two wines with similar body and tannin structure will feel familiar even when the flavors are completely different. Each guide above does this diagnostic work for the six wines we get asked about most.

**Q. What makes two wines similar to each other?**
A. Structure, much more than flavor. Two wines can both smell like cherries and drink completely differently. One light and tart, the other heavy and jammy, one dry and one almost sweet. The things that actually make a wine feel familiar are body, tannin, acidity, and how the fruit is weighted. Wines that share most of those characteristics will feel like natural companions even when they come from different countries and taste different on the surface.

**Q. Is there a way to find a cheaper version of an expensive wine I love?**
A. Usually yes, though you need to diagnose what you love about the expensive bottle first. Most expensive wines are priced for one specific reason: a famous appellation, a particular producer, the grape's scarcity, or the vintage. If you can identify what you love (the structure, the mineral quality, the fruit profile), you can often find that same characteristic in a wine from a less prestigious region at a fraction of the cost. Our Cabernet guide routes Napa drinkers toward Carménère and Rioja. Our Pinot Noir guide puts Mencía alongside bottles that cost twice as much and lose the comparison.

**Q. What should I try if I've been drinking the same one or two wines for years?**
A. Pick the guide that matches your current go-to and start with the first recommendation. We ordered every guide by structural similarity, so the first wine is always the closest match: familiar enough to actually enjoy, different enough to feel like discovery. The goal for that first bottle isn't to challenge yourself. It's to build confidence. Once it works, the next wine on the list is a small step further. That's the whole strategy.

**Q. Are the wines in these guides expensive or hard to find?**
A. We focused on everyday wines, not prestige chasing. The podcast has always covered wines under $25 that are relatively easy to find at a decent wine shop, Total Wine, Trader Joe's, or Costco. Most of what we recommend fits that profile. We don't suggest wines we can't actually buy, and we don't give good ratings to wines that don't earn them. On our 1 to 10 scale, a 7 means we'd buy it again. Anything under a 7, we say so clearly, and it doesn't make the guide.
