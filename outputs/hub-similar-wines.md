# If You Like This Wine, Try That: Your Guide to Finding Similar Wines You'll Love

You found a wine you love. Now the hard part: finding more like it.

The wine aisle is not designed to help you. Bottles are organized by region, by grape, by price, or by whatever the distributor paid for shelf space. None of that tells you whether the wine you're holding is going to taste anything like the one you drank last Saturday. The result is that most people stick to the same two or three wines and never explore. Not because they don't want to, but because there's no obvious path forward.

We built this guide to fix that. We're Joe and Carmela Mele, the independent husband-and-wife wine podcast (one of Apple Podcasts' Top 100 Food Podcasts), and we've spent years tasting affordable wines, giving them really honest ratings and reviews, and figuring out what connects them. We buy all our own wine and accept no free samples or sponsorships, which means our recommendations come from what we actually drink, not from what we've been paid to promote. The guides below cover the six wines we get asked about most. Each one identifies the best similar bottles and explains why they work.

## Key Questions We Answer

- How do you find wines similar to one you already like?
- What should I try if I love Pinot Noir but want something different?
- Is there a cheaper wine that drinks like Cabernet Sauvignon?
- What makes two wines similar in the first place?
- How do you branch out from your usual wine without wasting money on a bad bottle?

## If You Love Pinot Noir

Pinot Noir drinkers are some of the most loyal in the wine world, and once they realize how many other wines share Pinot's light touch, they tend to become the most adventurous. Our Pinot Noir guide covers Mencía, Beaujolais, Sangiovese, and three more reds that match Pinot's combination of bright cherry, soft tannin, and the food-friendliness that works across a full dinner rather than just a single course.

## If You Love Malbec

Malbec is where a lot of red wine drinkers start, and for good reason: it's bold but not punishing, dark-fruited, and relatively easy to find at almost every price point. If you've been drinking it for a while and want to branch out without losing what you love, our Malbec guide covers the best next bottles, including two alternatives that regularly come in under $15.

[Wines Similar to Malbec](https://thewinepairpodcast.com/blog/wines-similar-to-malbec)

## If You Love Cabernet Sauvignon

Cab drinkers often feel trapped between "I love this wine" and "I can't afford to drink it every night." Our Cabernet guide covers wines that match Cab's structure (the tannin, the dark fruit, the backbone) while broadening your options and lowering your per-bottle cost in most cases. Carménère and Rioja both make strong appearances.

[Wines Similar to Cabernet Sauvignon](https://thewinepairpodcast.com/blog/wines-similar-to-cabernet-sauvignon)

## If You Love Chardonnay

Chardonnay has a reputation problem, mostly caused by bad Chardonnay. If you like it, whether it's the body, the stone fruit, or the oak, there are wines that feel familiar while taking you somewhere new. Our Chardonnay guide covers the full range: wines that match the rich California style and wines that taste like a leaner, more energetic version of the same idea.

[Wines Similar to Chardonnay](https://thewinepairpodcast.com/blog/wines-similar-to-chardonnay)

## If You Love Sauvignon Blanc

Sauvignon Blanc drinkers tend to know exactly what they want: crisp, dry, and a little citrusy. The good news is that those characteristics show up across France, Italy, Spain, and Portugal in wines that are relatively easy to find and, in several cases, better value than the Sauvignon Blancs sitting next to them on the shelf. Our guide covers six bottles that share Sauvignon Blanc's energy without simply copying it.

[Wines Similar to Sauvignon Blanc](https://thewinepairpodcast.com/blog/wines-similar-to-sauvignon-blanc)

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q. How do you find wines similar to one you like?**
A. Start with structure, not flavor. Figure out what you actually love about your go-to wine. Is it the weight? The tannin? The acidity? The fruit profile? Wines that share those structural elements will feel familiar even if the specific flavors are different. The individual guides above walk through this for the six wines we get asked about most.

**Q. What makes two wines similar to each other?**
A. Structure more than flavor. Two wines can both taste like cherry and still drink completely differently. One might be light and tart, the other heavy and jammy. The characteristics that make wines feel alike are body, tannin level, acidity, and the weight of the fruit. Wines that match on most of those dimensions will feel familiar even if they come from opposite ends of the wine world.

**Q. Is there a way to find a cheaper version of an expensive wine I love?**
A. Usually yes. Most expensive wines are expensive because of one or two specific factors: a famous appellation, a legendary producer, or a low-yield vintage. If you can identify what you love about the expensive bottle (the structure, the fruit style, the mineral character), you can often find that same characteristic in a wine from a less famous region at a fraction of the cost. Our Cabernet and Pinot Noir guides both cover specific cheaper alternatives that deliver comparable structure.

**Q. What should I try if I only drink one or two wines and want to branch out?**
A. Go one step at a time. Pick the guide above that matches your current go-to and start with the first recommendation on the list. We ordered every guide by similarity to the original, so the first pick is always the closest match. If that wine works for you, the next one on the list is a bit further out.

**Q. Are the wines in these guides expensive or hard to find?**
A. We focused on everyday wines, not prestige chasing. Most of what we recommend is under $25 and relatively easy to find at a decent wine shop or a well-stocked grocery store. We don't suggest wines we can't actually buy.
